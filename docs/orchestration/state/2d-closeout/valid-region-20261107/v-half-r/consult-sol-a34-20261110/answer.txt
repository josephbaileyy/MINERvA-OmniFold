**I recommend option (i) at s = 0.2, but with intrinsically unresolvable rows recorded as structural failures, rather than automatically exempted.** That is the strongest defensible interpretation of “genuine two-dimensional information”: neither coordinate may be almost entirely integrated away at a given value of the other.

Option (i) directly requires substantial conditional population in both coordinates. Option (ii) preserves balanced **marginal** divisions, which is weaker: both margins can be balanced while almost all events occupy two opposite quadrants. A balanced frame therefore need not provide substantial information across both coordinates locally. Conversely, (i) permits useful staggered boundaries without requiring a common straight edge. Option (iii) leaves the identified near-strip defect intact; disclosure describes that weakness but does not repair it. These distinctions follow from the proposed definitions and acceptance rules. (A:114–163,165–182.)

**The draft overstates what monotonicity buys.** With fixed denominators and nonnegative cell weights, the largest-piece share cannot decrease under merging. Its 80% cap is therefore a sound monotone veto. But “two pieces each above 20%” is not equivalent to that cap: 70/10/10/10 passes the veto and fails the final requirement. The two-piece condition can first become satisfied as small pieces merge, then become unsatisfied when qualifying pieces merge together. Thus option (i) is well defined as a construction rule plus a terminal check; it is not a guarantee that greedy construction preserves eventual feasibility. Require finite, nonnegative weights explicitly for the monotonicity argument. (A:120–138.)

Option (ii)’s surviving-edge condition is monotone: once crossed, an edge cannot return. Nevertheless, greedy choices can destroy all partitions that would also satisfy response, support and bin count. Deterministic tie-breaking makes that outcome reproducible, not globally optimal. Neither option eliminates such corners, and the declaration correctly limits failure to **this procedure**, rather than proving no acceptable partition exists. (A:149–155,190–198; D:216–217.)

**The exemption is my main objection.** It correctly identifies one impossibility, but waiving it removes the resolution guarantee precisely where the simulation says one fine cell dominates. There is no declared limit on how much candidate weight may be exempt. It also misses other impossibilities: an ordered row with weights 19/62/19 cannot produce two contiguous pieces above 20%, although no cell exceeds 80%. I would identify such structural failures before merging and retain them as failures of the stipulated resolution requirement. If Joseph retains exemptions, the resulting claim must explicitly exclude those rows and columns, with their total candidate-weight fractions disclosed. (A:121–138,174–178.)

The numerical failure risk is unknown: candidate binnings and threshold comparisons were not computed in the declaration. Option (iii) allows the most joins; both substantive alternatives can block repairs. However, the assertion that (ii) necessarily terminates less often than (i) is unjustified: their allowed partitions are not nested, as the staircase example itself demonstrates. Correlations and sparse tails are plausible pressure points, not measured failure probabilities. (D:56–61; A:139–163.)
